Topic: antibiotic stewardship and the WHO AWaRe framework.

Scope: a portfolio exercise built only from public WHO and UN documents.

1 · The approved claims

Every sentence in every channel below is tagged to one of these. Anything not on this list is not said.

C1
WHO’s AWaRe classification groups antibiotics into Access, Watch and Reserve. It was developed in 2017 by the WHO Expert Committee on the Selection and Use of Essential Medicines.WHO Bulletin, online first, Nov 2025 (blt.25.293893)
C2
Reserve antibiotics are last-resort options for highly selected patients with confirmed or suspected life-threatening infections due to multidrug-resistant bacteria, to be used only when other options have failed or are unsuitable.WHO Bulletin, online first, Nov 2025; WHO AWaRe definitions
C3
The 2024 UN political declaration on AMR targets at least 70% of overall human antibiotic use globally from the Access group by 2030, and a 10% cut in global deaths associated with bacterial AMR, against the 2019 estimate of 4.95 million deaths.WHA78/8 summarising UNGA resolution 79/2
C4
WHO’s analysis of 2022 GLASS data found that only one in three reporting countries, territories and areas met the 70% Access target; Watch use remained high.WHO news release, 29 Apr 2025
C5
Watch antibiotics contribute disproportionately to resistance; WHO calls for stewardship policies so prescribers default to Access where possible and avoid unnecessary antibiotic use.WHO news release, 29 Apr 2025

Deliberately left out: India-specific figures, named drugs, doses and any treatment recommendation.

Why it’s built this way

  1. Subject and preheader: two subject lines for an A/B test (question vs. statistic). The preheader adds information instead of repeating the subject.
  2. Hero statistic: one sourced number above the fold, so the message lands even if nothing else is read (C4).
  3. Three-pill strip: scannable on a phone, and every descriptor comes from an approved claim (C1, C2, C3, C5).
  4. One call to action: a single button to the primary source, no competing links.
  5. Numbered references and a compliance footer: date of preparation and an audience line, as in real HCP emails.
  6. Single column, 600px: renders cleanly on mobile; body text at 15px; a button large enough to tap.

Channel rules applied: numbered references, one CTA, no efficacy or treatment claims, no brand mention.

Why the right antibiotic matters

Antibiotics are not all the same. Doctors choose from different groups C1, and some are saved as a last resort for serious, life-threatening infections caused by resistant bacteria, when other options have failed or are unsuitable C2. Avoiding unnecessary antibiotic use helps slow resistance C5.

If you have questions about your antibiotic, ask your doctor or pharmacist.

Channel rules applied: no drug names, no dosing, no instruction to start or stop treatment, referral to a professional.

3 · How I would keep AI-assisted content safe

  1. Source first. Claims are written and verified before any drafting. AI only adapts approved claims; it never introduces new ones.
  2. Traceability. Each sentence carries a claim ID that links back to a dated, citable source.
  3. Channel rules. Each channel has its own limits on length, tone and what must not be said.
  4. Human checks. A pharmacist verifies claims and numbers, and a native speaker reviews each translation before anything goes out.
  5. Review trail. A log of who checked what and when, so changes are auditable.
  6. Formal review. Final content goes through the organisation’s medical, legal and regulatory review.
